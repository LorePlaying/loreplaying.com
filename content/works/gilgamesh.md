## Story and context

The Epic of Gilgamesh survives in several ancient Mesopotamian versions, most fully in an Akkadian poem preserved on clay tablets. Its central figure is the king of Uruk, whose strength and authority initially make him a troubling ruler. The gods answer the city's complaints by creating Enkidu, a wild man who first challenges Gilgamesh and then becomes his closest companion.

Together they seek fame in the Cedar Forest and confront its guardian, Humbaba. Their victories do not protect them from loss: after Enkidu dies, Gilgamesh leaves the security of his kingdom and searches for a way to escape death. He meets Utnapishtim, the survivor of a great flood, but learns that immortality is not a prize he can claim.

## Reading the journey

The epic turns an outward expedition into a meditation on friendship, grief, and the limits of human life. Gilgamesh returns to Uruk changed, recognizing the city and its walls as a lasting human achievement. The map follows the literary sequence, but the Cedar Forest and Mount Mashu belong to a mythic geography rather than securely identified modern sites. Dilmun is represented by a traditional regional identification.
