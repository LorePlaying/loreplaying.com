## Story and context

The Iliad is an ancient Greek epic traditionally attributed to Homer. It does not narrate the whole Trojan War: its action concentrates on a short, decisive period late in the conflict. The poem begins with the anger of Achilles after his quarrel with Agamemnon, then follows the consequences for the Greek army and the defenders of Troy.

The conflict is shaped by competing obligations: personal honor, loyalty to companions, family, and the demands of the gods. As the fighting intensifies, the poem shifts between the Greek camp and the Trojan city, giving weight to both sides. The final encounter between Achilles and Hector leads not to a simple victory celebration, but to a meeting between two grieving fathers.

## Reading the itinerary

The map adds places associated with the wider war tradition, including Sparta, Mycenae, and Aulis, before reaching Troy. These points frame the expedition; they should not be mistaken for a complete travel narrative contained within the poem itself. Troy is associated with the archaeological site at Hisarlik, while the route lines are schematic rather than a verified reconstruction of Bronze Age movements.
