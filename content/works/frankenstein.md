## Story and context

Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* (1818) is framed through the letters of Arctic explorer Robert Walton. Walton's account encloses Victor Frankenstein's confession: a young student of natural philosophy, Victor becomes obsessed with discovering the principle of life and assembles a living being from dead matter. Horrified by the Creature's appearance, Victor abandons him.

The Creature must make sense of a world that rejects him. By observing the De Lacey family, he learns language and develops a desire for companionship. When he approaches them, they drive him away. His loneliness hardens into anger, and his encounter with Victor becomes a demand that his creator make a companion for him.

Victor begins the second creation but destroys it before it is complete. The Creature retaliates against Victor's loved ones, while Victor pursues him across Europe and into the Arctic. Walton's ship eventually finds Victor near death; after Victor dies, the Creature appears and grieves over the person he has spent the novel pursuing.

## Themes and significance

The novel asks what responsibilities follow from creation and discovery. Victor's ambition is not treated as an isolated scientific achievement: his refusal to care for the life he makes sets much of the tragedy in motion. The Creature, meanwhile, complicates the boundary between human and monster by showing how neglect, exclusion, and the need for connection shape his choices.

Shelley also makes storytelling part of the novel's design. Walton records Victor's account, and Victor recounts the Creature's history, so readers encounter each character through another person's narration. This layered structure raises questions about sympathy, judgment, and whose account of events is believed.

## Reading the journey

The story moves from Victor's home in Geneva to his studies in Ingolstadt, through the Alpine landscape, across parts of continental Europe and the British Isles, and finally into the Arctic. These settings follow the novel's narrative sequence; regional or loosely identified waypoints should not be read as a precise travel itinerary.
