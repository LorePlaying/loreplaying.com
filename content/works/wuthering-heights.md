## Story and context

Emily Brontë's only novel traces the intertwined histories of the Earnshaw and Linton families in a fictional Yorkshire landscape. The account is framed by Mr Lockwood, a new tenant at Thrushcross Grange, and Nelly Dean, the housekeeper who relates the events that shaped the neighboring households.

Heathcliff arrives at Wuthering Heights as a child and grows close to Catherine Earnshaw. When Catherine marries Edgar Linton, Heathcliff leaves and later returns with wealth and a plan for revenge. His actions bind the two families together through coercion, inheritance, and loss; the consequences pass to the next generation, especially Catherine Linton and Hareton Earnshaw.

The narrative does not unfold in a simple chronological order. Lockwood's brief experience of the Heights opens a much longer history told by Nelly, whose memories and judgments shape what the reader learns. The shifting frame makes the past feel both intimate and difficult to verify.

## Themes and significance

The novel examines how class, property, and social acceptance determine who is allowed to belong. Heathcliff's exclusion helps explain his bitterness, but Brontë also shows how he turns that injury into deliberate harm. The moors and the contrast between the exposed Heights and the more sheltered Grange reinforce the characters' competing ideas of freedom, security, and home.

The destructive cycle is not resolved by a simple victory. The younger Catherine and Hareton gradually build a relationship different from the one that shaped their parents, suggesting that care and education can interrupt inherited hostility. Their change gives the ending a measure of hope without erasing the damage that came before.
