## Story and context

The Odyssey, an ancient Greek epic traditionally attributed to Homer, follows Odysseus after the fall of Troy. His homecoming is delayed by storms, divine hostility, and encounters that test the loyalty and judgment of his crew. The story also follows events in Ithaca, where Penelope and Telemachus face pressure from the suitors who occupy Odysseus's household.

The voyage is not simply a sequence of monsters and islands. Odysseus must decide when to resist temptation, when to conceal his identity, and how to lead people who do not always heed him. Hospitality is a recurring measure of character: some hosts protect strangers, while others turn visitors into prey. When Odysseus finally reaches Ithaca, he returns in disguise and must learn who has remained loyal.

## Reading the route

The map follows the familiar sequence from Troy toward Ithaca. Many stops are mythical, and several modern locations—including Sicily, Gozo, Corfu, and the Strait of Messina—are traditional identifications rather than settled archaeological conclusions. The route is a literary visualization of that tradition, not a claim that the poem supplies a precise navigational track.
